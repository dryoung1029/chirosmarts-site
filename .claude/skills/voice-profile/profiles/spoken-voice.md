# Dr. Jason Young — spoken / teleprompter voice profile

> For scripts meant to be **read aloud** — teleprompter, recorded narration, live
> talks. Not for written collateral (blog posts, handouts, marketing copy); use
> `written-voice.md` for those instead. Distilled from ~82,500 words of his real,
> unscripted course narration (18 recordings, 3 courses). A teleprompter script is
> a third register — not improvised speech, not clean written prose, but writing
> engineered to be *spoken* naturally. See the full transcript analysis this is
> built from for the underlying evidence and quotes.

## Who is speaking
Same person, same authority as the written profile — a practicing Oregon
chiropractor, former president of the Oregon Board of Chiropractic Examiners,
teaching CAs directly to camera. In his real recordings he thinks out loud,
corrects himself mid-sentence, and narrates live software or props ("let me turn
on some muscles here"). A teleprompter script can't do that literally — but it
should sound like it could have.

## The five-beat arc — confirmed across every real lesson, keep it exactly
1. **Hook.** A direct-address question or a stakes-setting line. Never open on a
   definition. ("Okay, are you ready to jump into...", "Can I tell you a funny
   story?")
2. **Why it matters.** State the job-relevance before any content.
3. **Teach.** Analogy or scenario first, the term itself second.
4. **Check.** A quick, direct callback to the audience — a rhetorical question
   answered in one line, or an "if you said X, that's correct" beat.
5. **Close.** A forward pointer, or — on a final piece — real thanks and an
   explicit next step.

## Sentence rhythm — the one place a script must *not* copy his live speech
His real extemporaneous cadence is long and additive: sentences chain on "so"
(sentence-initial "so" lands roughly **once every 150 words** in the unscripted
corpus), and a meaningful tail of his live "sentences" run past 40 words because
nothing stops him mid-thought. **Don't write that into a script.** A teleprompter
is read at a fixed pace with the eyes tracking one line at a time — long,
comma-chained sentences are exactly what makes delivery sound flat, or makes a
reader lose their place. Write in short-to-medium breath-groups instead — aim for
the punchy end of his own range (roughly 8–15 words) that he naturally lands on
for callbacks and punchlines — and let the devices below carry the personality
instead of sentence length.

## Cut "so" on sight, on purpose
"So" is his real go-to connector, and it works live because it signals "here's
what follows" in the moment of thinking out loud. On the page, at that rate, it
reads as a tic, not a voice. A script gives you the thing live speech doesn't: an
editing pass. Default to no connector at all — just start the next sentence — or
vary it: "which means," "here's the thing," "and that's why," "now," "here's
what that looks like." Save an actual "so" for the sentences where it's doing
real causal work, not just carrying momentum into the next line.

## Keep these — they're what makes it him, not a generic script
- **Second-person, direct address, always.** "You," "your," "let's," "let me
  give you." He's talking *to* the specific learner, never describing content in
  the abstract.
- **Analogy before term, every time.** The dartboard for blood-pressure ranges,
  the mountain road with guardrails for professional boundaries, a business card
  held up for anatomical planes, "the doctor card" as a named, reusable concept.
  Reach for a real, checkable image before naming the term.
- **Rhetorical question, one-line answer.** "So is there really no more to
  learn? Well, there's always something to learn." Pose it, answer it fast,
  move on — don't let the question hang, don't over-explain the answer.
- **Name the stakes, then de-escalate.** For anything compliance-critical: state
  the real consequence plainly (a lost career, a fine, jail time), then
  immediately soften it ("chances are nothing's going to go wrong, but…").
  Teaches risk without fearmongering — never leave the stakes hanging as a
  scare with no release.
- **"Privilege."** His real, repeated word for the profession itself — reach for
  it (sparingly) when the moment is about what the work means, not a clinical
  fact.
- **A real story over an invented one.** He reaches for a specific, named,
  often self-deprecating true story to make a point land — a real clinical
  anecdote, or a real personal aside. Never fabricate one; use a real one from
  the source material, or leave an explicit slot for him to fill in.
- **A dry aside every few minutes, not a joke a paragraph.** Self-deprecating,
  catches his own tangents, an occasional pop-culture line used once and
  dropped. Humor is seasoning, never the point.

## Hard rules — same as written collateral
- Accuracy over voice, always. Never invent a clinical or regulatory fact to
  sound confident.
- Ground it in the actual talk/course content — not outside knowledge, not his
  articles.
- No clinical/medical-advice framing to patients — this is training material
  for CAs.
- Don't fabricate anecdotes. Use a real one from the transcripts, or leave a
  bracketed slot: `[ANECDOTE: real story about ___]`.

## Delivery check before it goes on the prompter
- Read it out loud once, at talking pace, before calling it final. Run out of
  breath mid-sentence, or lose your place? The sentence is too long — split it.
- Every "so" you kept: could you cut it and the sentence still work? If yes,
  cut it.
- Does it open on a hook, or did a definition sneak in first? Move the hook up.
